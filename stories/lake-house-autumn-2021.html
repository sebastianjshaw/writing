Lake House, Autumn 2021

At 2am a bruised stranger from another Earth lands in Tony Stark’s back garden, wearing a face Tony knows.

By Sebastian Shaw

The crash came at 2am. FRIDAY kept her voice low so she wouldn’t wake Morgan.

“Boss, spatial tear, two hundred metres north. One life sign. Human, mostly.”

“Mostly,” Tony repeated. He was already on the porch steps, nanites crawling up his right arm into a gauntlet. “Love that. Great word. Very reassuring.”

He found the man in a crater of flattened ferns: red and blue suit, a big black spider across the chest, half the mask ripped away. He was older, mid-thirties, with lines round the eyes. It was the same face.

Tony’s gauntlet powered down without him telling it to.

The man sat up, looked at him and laughed. There was nothing friendly in it.

“Of course. Of course it’s you. Reed opens a door into the infinite and I land in Tony Stark’s back garden.” He pulled off what was left of the mask. “Which one are you? The one who built a prison in the Negative Zone for his friends, or the one who cloned Thor and had the clone kill Bill Foster? Wait, sorry. Same guy.”

“I don’t know what any of that means.”

“No?” Peter studied him for a moment. “Different Earth, then. Same haircut. Same goofy beard.” He stood up, wincing. “You looked at me like you’d seen a ghost.”

Tony said nothing for a long time. Then: “There was a kid. Peter. Fifteen when I found him.”

“Found him.”

“On YouTube. Stopping a car. I flew to Queens, sat on his aunt’s sofa, lied to her about a grant, and took him to Germany to fight Captain America.”

Peter’s expression didn’t change, which was worse than if it had.

“Was Steve working for Hydra? Or was it just Tony Stark fighting good men because he knows best again?”

“There were Accords. A hundred and seventeen countries signed. Steve went rogue to protect a friend who...” Tony stopped. “Barnes killed my parents.”

“Did you know that at the airport?”

A pause. “No.”

“Then that’s not why you went.” Peter tilted his head. “So you went to arrest a good man for disagreeing with you, and you took a fifteen-year-old along to help.”

“He was good. He was better than good, he...”

“I teach kids that age. Taught, anyway. Midtown High, chemistry.” Peter’s voice stayed perfectly level. “If a billionaire turned up at one of my students’ homes, lied to the guardian and flew the kid abroad to brawl with a super-soldier, I wouldn’t call it mentorship. I’d call the police.”

“It wasn’t like that.”

“It’s always exactly like that. That’s the trick with you. You do it, and then you explain why it was different this time.”

Tony’s jaw tightened. “You don’t know me.”

“I’ve known you twenty years. Different face, same instincts. Let me guess the rest.” Peter started counting on his fingers. “You built him a suit. Not because he asked for one, but because it was easier to own him than to trust him. You put a tracker in it. Probably a kill switch, or something you told yourself wasn’t one. And then something big came along, and he followed you into it, because that’s what you’d trained him to do.”

The woods were silent.

“Space,” Tony said eventually. “He stowed away on a ship. I could have sent him home. I made him an Avenger instead. Then half the universe got snapped out of existence, and he...” He stopped. “He held on to me. He said he didn’t want to go. He kept saying sorry.”

Peter let that sit for a while. When he spoke again, there was no sympathy in his voice.

“He said sorry to you.”

“Yes.”

“Then he learned that from you, too. Kids apologise when they’ve been taught the adult’s feelings are the thing that matters.”

Tony actually stepped back.

“You want me to say it wasn’t your fault,” Peter said. “I won’t. Grief isn’t penance, Tony. You don’t get to feel terrible and call the account settled. My Tony does monstrous things on purpose. He plans them, he signs off on them, and then he tells you it was necessary. You’re different. You do it by accident, as a side effect of being certain. I’ve never worked out which of the two is worse.”

“I’ve saved the world.”

“You’ve saved it from things you built. Go on, count them.” Peter waited. “Who was the last guy who tried to kill you? Did he have a grudge? Was it his company you put out of business? His research you laughed at? His city you dropped out of the sky?”

Tony didn’t answer, and that was answer enough.

“Every Tony makes his own villains,” Peter said. “Mine does it industrially. And somewhere out there, right now, there’s a man you humiliated once and have already forgotten. When he comes, he won’t come for you. He’ll come for whatever you left behind. Whoever you handed your toys to.”

Tony thought of a stage at MIT, a hologram, and some engineer he’d brushed off in a corridor. He couldn’t put a name to the face.

“You’re wrong about one thing,” Tony said quietly. “I didn’t want to own him. I wanted... I never had anyone who...”

“I know. You wanted a son, you didn’t ask him, and you didn’t ask his aunt. You made a fifteen-year-old responsible for filling a hole in you.” Peter’s voice softened, but only slightly. “Look, the man I looked up to was Reed Richards. Reed got it wrong in our war too. He backed registration and he helped build that prison. But Reed was wrong because he trusted the maths. You’re wrong because you trust yourself. Reed shows you his working. You never show anyone anything until it’s already finished and already flying.”

The air behind him rippled. A thin, precise voice came through it: “Peter. Thirty seconds. Please don’t argue with anyone.”

“Too late,” Peter muttered.

He walked towards the rift and stopped at the edge of it.

“If you ever get the chance to fix it,” he said, without turning round, “to bring them back, or him back, you’ll take it. Whatever it costs, you’ll decide the price for everyone else, and you won’t ask a single one of them.” He glanced over his shoulder. “Just this once, ask somebody first.”

“And if it works?”

“Then he’ll come home and thank you, because he’s a good kid.” Peter’s mouth twisted. “He loved you. That isn’t to your credit, Tony. It’s to his.”

Then the rift closed, and he was gone.

❧

Tony stood in the cold until FRIDAY asked if he wanted the porch lights on. He didn’t answer her. He went inside, walked past the photo on the shelf without looking at it, and sat in the dark until Morgan woke up.
