Merlin, Thursday

A day in the life of a large, clumsy dog, told through his nose.

By Sebastian Shaw

The first thought of the day is the same as the last thought of yesterday, which is: food?

It is dark. Father is a long shape under the duvet making the noise he makes when he is not yet a person. Merlin doesn’t really know the word Father. Merlin has his own name for him, which is not a word but a smell, and is the best smell.

Merlin puts his chin on the edge of the bed. This is subtle. This is forty kilos of subtle, breathing directly onto Father’s face at a distance of four centimetres, and it works, as it always works, because Father opens one eye and says the word that means no but in the tone that means fine.

Breakfast is the same as yesterday. It is the best breakfast he has ever had. Father stands at the counter while Merlin eats, holding his coffee with both hands and saying “good job, Spoops” every so often, as if eating breakfast were an achievement. Merlin agrees that it is, an important one.

❧

Then the walk!

Merlin’s eyes have never been much use for colour. The world they show him is washed out, blues and yellows and greys, a street left out in the rain. His nose is a different matter. His nose sees everything, in bright swirls. Merlin has never seen The Starry Night, but if he had, and if he could understand art and metaphor, then the vast sweeping strokes of colour across the vaults of heaven would be something a little like how he smells the world.

Andra Långgatan at seven in the morning is a fresh canvas, painted overnight by strangers, and it is still wet. The falafel outside the bar on the corner is a thick smear of hot orange, with something muted and lavender running through it where whoever ate it was sad. The fox is a single streak of rust from the bins to the gutter, so bright and so recent it almost hums. Outrageous. A fox in his city, the nerve. The lamppost is layer on layer of colour, thick and heavy like oil paints, every dog painting over the last, and at the top, fresh this morning, is the small dog with the loud opinion: a furious little splash of yellow. Merlin paints over it.

He glances at the falafel in his subtle way. He would like to roll in the place where the falafel was, but Father says his name in the voice with corners on it. No matter how innocently he looks at something good to roll in, Father always seems to know his intention. So he files the falafel under later and knows, with total certainty, that later will not come.

The tram goes past, enormous and indifferent. Merlin has decided to forgive the tram. It rarely stops for him and he has stopped taking it personally. When it does stop, it often takes him somewhere exciting and strange.

Father takes him down the slope that means van.

Merlin loves the van with the specific love of something that only ever takes him to good places. He rides up front, because he has to keep Father safe from other dogs or people who might rush up to the van, sitting upright in the seat. He keeps his head pressed to the side window and leaves a smear that is a kind of signature. Every so often he turns to look at Father, to check Father is still driving correctly. Father reaches across at the lights without looking and finds Merlin’s ear, every time, as if it were the gearstick.

The city comes in through the gap at the top of the window in long ribbons of colour. Bread is gold. Diesel is a dirty purple that sits at the back of his throat. Under everything, always somewhere to the left, is the sea: a cold deep blue, the colour the whole city is painted on.

The van has a voice that lives in it. It belongs to a man who is never there, and it comes out of the dashboard, low and patient, telling a story that never ends. Sometimes the voice gets quiet and slow and Father leans forward slightly. Father never answers it, and it never stops for Merlin. But Father’s face does something while it talks. It goes far away, to a place that isn’t the van or the road, and wherever that place is, it seems to be a good one.

Then the long boring grey. There’s nothing to see, just the same slice of road over and over. Merlin lies out on the seat. Today the seat is long. Sometimes it isn’t. Today is a good day. Merlin drifts off to sleep.

Then the turn. The particular turn. Merlin knows this turn the way he knows his own back leg, and he sits up on the seat and begins to make a noise that starts in his chest and has nowhere to go.

“I know, buddy, we’re almost there,” Father says. “But please sit back down so I can see the mirror.”

Merlin doesn’t know what the mirror is. Father asks about it every day. Perhaps one day he will learn.

Father jabs at the dashboard and the man in the van stops mid-sentence. “I know, Stinky,” he says. “I know.”

❧

Then a door, and behind the door, everything happens at once. There is the tall woman who smells of liver treats and other dogs’ ears. There is Bosse, a Staffie built like a breeze block, who greets Merlin by running into him at full speed, which is correct. There is a nervous whippet called Elsa who is new and does not yet understand that Merlin is a lapdog. Merlin explains this by trying to sit on her. She leaves. He thinks this went well.

The middle of the day is a long golden blur of being a dog among dogs. There is a ball. There is a different ball, which is better because Bosse has it. There is a tug rope that Merlin and a Labrador named Sissi hold between them for eleven minutes without either of them moving, an act of pure faith. There is water, drunk so enthusiastically that a third of it ends up in his beard and carries on being drunk for the next half hour, in small cold drips, onto whoever is nearby.

Then the nap. Fourteen dogs on the floor of one room, breathing at different speeds until somehow they are breathing at the same speed. Merlin dreams about the falafel.

He wakes once, in the middle of the afternoon, and Father is not there.

He knows Father is not there. Father is never there at this time. This is not news. But there is a moment, every day, between asleep and awake, when he forgets the rule, and the room is full of the wrong smells, and he lifts his head and checks the door. The door is a door. Nobody comes through it. Merlin puts his head back down on Sissi, who allows this, and goes back to sleep, and the moment closes over like water.

❧

The van comes back when the light is gone.

He hears it before anyone else does. He hears it from the other side of the building, through a wall, under the noise of thirteen other dogs, because it is the only engine in the world. By the time the tall woman opens the door he has already been standing at it for some time, quivering, a large furry thing composed entirely of the present tense.

Father crouches, which is a mistake. Merlin has been told about jumping. He has been told many times, in the voice with corners on it, and he knows exactly what the voice means. None of that is available to him right now. He arrives at Father from all directions at once, and Father ends up sitting on the floor with forty kilos of dog in his lap.

“Yes,” Father says, from the floor. “Yes. Hey buddy. Enough. OK.”

Father smells of the office, which is coffee and carpet and a great many other people, and of the van, and under that of himself, which is a warm red-brown, the colour of the inside of the van on a sunny day, and the one Merlin measures every other colour against. Merlin cleans the office off his hands as best he can. It takes a while. There is a lot of office.

❧

At home there is dinner, which is the same as breakfast, and also the best dinner he has ever had. Then the evening walk to the big park with the hill, Slottsskogen, where the trees hold a different painting entirely. The deer have left broad, slow strokes of moss green across the path. Every trunk is flecked with squirrel, sharp and nervous, little dots of electric white. The wet leaves are every colour of autumn at once, and they are mixing as he watches.

A squirrel runs up a trunk and looks down at him with open contempt. Merlin looks at a very interesting patch of grass in the opposite direction until it goes away. He is not frightened of squirrels.

Father stands under a lamp and looks at his phone and then, after a while, doesn’t. He just stands there, breathing out white, while Merlin studies the tree. Father looks the way Merlin feels after his day with his temporary pack: worn out from hours spent with lots of other animals, and pleased about it.

Later Father sits on the sofa with the laptop open and the television on and is in neither of them. Merlin climbs onto the sofa, which he is not allowed to do, and lies across Father’s legs, all forty kilos of him, until the laptop has to be put down.

“You’re not a small dog,” Father says.

Merlin has heard this before. He has never found it persuasive.

The flat gets quiet. The street outside gets loud, then quiet again. Father’s hand finds the place on Merlin’s flank and stays there, not scratching, just resting, the way you’d rest a hand on something to make sure it was still there.

Merlin closes his eyes.

Food? he thinks, out of habit.

And then, because the hand is still there: no. This.
