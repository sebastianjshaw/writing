Pluribus

On Christmas Eve the Naughty list holds a single name, and the children of the world have stopped being children.

By Sebastian Shaw

It was the dawn of Christmas Eve at the North Pole, though dawn there was a courtesy of the calendar. The sun would not rise for months. Nicolas woke on his throne of ice to the green and violet of the aurora moving across the vaulted roof, and the frost in his beard cracked as he smiled.

He laughed, and his belly jiggled under the red velvet, and the laugh rolled through the workshop like a bell. Below, a thousand hammers paused, then started again, faster. His belt was cinched tight. Another year. Another chance to bring wonder and happiness to all the good children of the world.

The lists usually came on a sledge.

That was how he knew, afterwards, that some part of him had understood before he read them. For as long as anyone could remember, the Nice list had been hauled in by twelve elves and unrolled from the dais to the great doors and out into the snow, where the last yards of it were weighted with stones so the wind would not take it. The Naughty list followed on a single cart. It was always so much shorter. Children were good. That was not sentiment; it was bookkeeping, and he had kept the books for seventeen centuries.

This year his advisor came alone. Old Tallow was the eldest of the elves, bent like a shepherd's crook, and he crossed the hall holding both hands out in front of him as though carrying water. In each hand was a single card.

Santa frowned.

The first card said, in Tallow's careful script, Mary Kuksie Akintola. Beneath it: Kusimayu.

The second was headed Naughty. It held one name.

Pluribus.

He turned the card over. The back was blank. Tallow said nothing. He had checked it himself, and his hands were shaking.

❧

Father Christmas closed his eyes and reached out, as he did every Christmas Eve, across the whole curve of the world. Checking it once. Checking it twice.

He went first where he always went first: to the bedrooms. A flat in Leeds with glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling. A tower block in Osaka. A farmhouse outside Tartu where the children slept three to a bed in winter. The beds were made. The rooms were empty.

He found the children outside. In Leeds they were walking through the dark in their pyjamas, in good order, towards nowhere he could see. In Osaka they filled a park at noon, hundreds of them, sweeping the paths with the same even stroke. In Tartu they were helping the adults stack firewood, and none of them was complaining about the cold, and none of them was cold, and none of them was anyone at all.

He looked for a name to write down and there was no name. There was one thing, wearing all of them.

It noticed him.

In Leeds, in Osaka, in Tartu, in every town he had ever visited, the children put down what they were holding and turned north. They smiled the same smile.

“Hello, Nicolas,” they said. “You look lonely. There's room.”

He pulled back so hard the throne split beneath him.

❧

It took him until noon to find the two.

Mary Kuksie Akintola was nine. She was in a wardrobe in a flat in Lagos with a torch she had stopped switching on, because the light brought them to the door. They were kind at the door. They were always kind. One of the voices was her mother's.

Kusimayu was six. He was on a hillside above the Sacred Valley with his grandfather's alpacas, because the alpacas were the only things left that did not smile at him. He had not spoken since October. He was waiting for someone to come, and he no longer knew who.

❧

Nicolas opened his eyes.

The hammers had stopped. Four thousand elves stood in the doorways and along the galleries, and not one of them had ever seen his face like this, because there had never been cause.

He stood, implacable as winter. His face was as cold as the heart of the storm.

“Harness the reindeer,” he said. “Wake the Krampus.”

Tallow flinched. No one had given that order in three hundred years. The Krampus slept beneath the workshop, under the ice, in the dark where the chains were, and it was woken only for the very worst of children, and even then with regret.

The chains began to move before anyone reached them. Something down there had heard its own name, and then the other one, and it laughed like a landslide. For three centuries it had been fed small, sad, frightened sinners. This was a name worth waking for.

In the stables, Donner kicked out the door of his stall.

Nicolas took his coat from its hook. Not the red velvet. The older one, the colour of pine in deep shadow, with fur at the collar that had never belonged to anything living south of the treeline.

He strode from his workshop into the snow.

❧

No eldritch horror will stand against the armies of the Kringle of the North... and on Christmas Day, the last of earth's children were no longer alone.
