Below the Ice

Three survivors cross a frozen plain in a car that swallows light, looking for a manhole cover forty paces north of a mast.

By Sebastian Shaw

The tunnel took the last of the daylight about a kilometre in. After that there were only the headlamps and the hiss of tyres on wet concrete, and the car, which made no sound of its own at all.

Ines drove. Callum sat beside her with the map on his knees, a sheet of squared paper someone had drawn in biro and folded so many times the creases had gone furry. Petra was in the back with her knees up and her coat zipped to the chin, watching the rear window.

Nobody had chosen the car. It had been in a garage in Harnstad with the keys on the seat and the doors open, and it had started, and that had been enough. It was black, but not the way paint is black. Callum said it was like sitting inside an oil slick. The surface seemed to move when you weren’t looking at it. Light from the tunnel lamps, where the lamps still worked, went into the bonnet and did not come out, and then, at some angle you could never predict, a single lamp would be thrown back whole and white into your eyes. Petra had stopped looking at it after the first day. She said it felt like the car was looking back.

“Four hundred metres,” Callum said. “Then it opens out.”

“Opens out to what?”

“Ice.”

The end of the tunnel was a grey square that grew without getting brighter. Then they were through it and the world was white in every direction, flat and hard, polished by wind into ridges like the ribs of something buried. The sky was the same colour as the ground. There was no horizon, only the place where you stopped being able to see.

Ines slowed. The tyres found the ice and held, barely.

“How far?”

Callum turned the map round. “Three kilometres. Bit less. There’s a hut, then a mast, then the cover is forty paces north of the mast. It says forty paces.”

“Whose paces?”

He didn’t answer. Nobody knew who had drawn the map. It had been pinned to the inside of the garage door with a nail through the corner, and underneath someone had written SAFE BELOW and underlined it three times, so hard the pen had torn the paper.

They drove. The car took the white glare and drank it, so that looking at the bonnet was like looking into a hole in the day. The wind pushed at them from the left, steadily, as if leaning on a door.

Petra saw it first. She didn’t scream. She said, “Ines,” in a voice so flat it took a moment to understand.

It came from the right, low over the ice. It did not drift. Ines had expected, if she had expected anything, that they would float the way things float in water, slow and lazy and borne along. This one moved in bursts. It would hang perfectly still for half a second and then hurl itself forward ten metres, stop dead, correct, hurl again. It moved like something being yanked on a cable by someone very strong and very angry.

It was the colour of blood orange, the inside of one, wet-looking. Its body was a rough sphere about the size of a man’s torso. Short tentacles hung from the underside in a tight bunch, flexing, opening and closing like a hand testing its grip. And on the front, taking up most of it, the eye: one eye, compound, faceted, a dull dark bronze that caught the light in a thousand small points.

“Drive,” Callum said.

Ines was already driving. The car lurched and slid and found purchase and the speedometer climbed. In the mirror the thing jerked, stopped, jerked. It was keeping pace. Then it was gaining.

“There’s another,” Petra said. “Two. Three.”

They came over a ridge to the left, low and fast, in that same violent stop-start, as if each movement cost them nothing and they could do it forever. Ines heard herself breathing through her teeth.

The first one hit the side of the car at the rear door. There was no bang. The car’s surface rippled outwards from the impact like a pond and the creature’s reflection went down into it, orange smeared through black, and for a moment Ines saw the thing twice: once pressed against the window, tentacles spread flat on the glass, and once inside the door panel, distorted, swimming. Petra was shoving herself across the back seat away from it.

Then the car died.

Not stalled. Died. The dashboard went dark, the engine note, which they had barely noticed, simply stopped, and they slid on in silence across the ice, turning slowly, until the front wheel caught a ridge and they stopped.

Nobody spoke. The thing on Petra’s window shifted. Its tentacles moved across the glass with small wet sounds. The eye was very close. Ines could see each facet, and in each facet a tiny dark car.

“The hut,” Callum said quietly. “Ines. The hut.”

It was perhaps two hundred metres away, a low prefab box half buried in drift, its door hanging open. Outside it, in a row, were three shapes that were too bright for this place. Red. Shockingly red, after so much white.

“What are those?”

“I don’t know. Does it matter?”

The other two creatures had stopped, hanging in the air ten metres off, perfectly still. Waiting. Or deciding.

“On three,” Callum said, and then didn’t count, just opened his door and ran.

Ines followed. She heard Petra’s door behind her and the slap of feet on ice and then nothing but her own breathing and the wind. She didn’t look back. She had learned not to look back, eleven days ago, on the bridge out of Harnstad, and it had cost someone else to teach her.

The red shapes were trikes. Low, fat-tyred, two wheels at the rear, battered and frost-rimed. Each had a single seat and wide handlebars. And on the back of each, behind the seat, rising out of the rear frame, was a bump: a rounded swelling in the metal, smooth and too big, like the lump on a cartoon character’s head after the anvil lands. It looked absurd. It looked like a joke.

Callum was already on one, stabbing at the controls. The engine coughed and caught. “There’s a button,” he shouted. “On the left grip. Red button.”

“What does it do?”

He pressed it.

The bump glowed. Not on the surface: inside it, as if the metal had turned to frosted glass with a light behind it. Then a ball of green came out of the back of it. There was no muzzle, no barrel, no hole. The energy simply passed through the steel as if the steel weren’t there, a fist-sized sphere of green so pure it hurt, and went out behind the trike at head height, fast and straight.

It hit nothing. It went on across the ice until it was a speck and then it was gone.

“It only fires backwards,” Callum said. He sounded like he might laugh. “It only fires backwards.”

Ines understood. There was no aiming it. The bump was welded to the frame and the frame pointed where the trike pointed. To shoot something you had to turn your back on it. You had to be running.

Petra reached them. Her face was grey. “They’re coming.”

They were. All three, now, and two more behind, low over the ice in their jolting bursts. The nearest stopped twenty metres away and hung. Its tentacles opened and closed.

“Get on,” Ines said.

They rode.

The trikes were faster than they looked and bucked over the ridges hard enough to slam Ines’s teeth together. The mast was visible now, a thin black line against the white, half a kilometre ahead. Behind them, Ines could hear nothing over the engine, which was worse than hearing something.

She pressed the button.

She felt it in her spine, a warmth that went through the seat, and saw the green light wash briefly across the ice on either side. She didn’t see what it hit. She pressed it again and again, blind, and on the third press there was a sound behind her, a deep wet crack like a frozen lake giving way, and something orange tumbled past on her left, end over end, and slid across the ice and lay still, its eye gone dark and dull as a stone.

Callum whooped. It was a horrible sound, high and cracked. Ines didn’t blame him.

Then Petra turned her head.

Ines saw her do it. Saw her look back over her shoulder to see what was behind, which was the one thing the trike could already see for her. The handlebars went with her. The trike swung right, side-on to the pursuit, and the green ball she fired at that moment went harmlessly out across the empty ice, and the creature that had been behind her was no longer behind her. It was beside her. Where the bump could not reach.

It did not slow down. It hit her at the height of her shoulders and the tentacles closed and both of them went off the trike together and the trike went on without her, riderless, curving gently away to the right, still firing green into nothing every few seconds because her hand must have locked the button as she fell.

Ines did not stop. She knew she would think about that later. She knew she would think about very little else.

❧

The mast. Forty paces north. Callum was off his trike before it stopped, on his knees, scraping at the drift with his gloves. Ines swung her trike round so its back faced the way they had come and sat on it and held the button down. The bump glowed and pulsed. Green went out across the ice in a stream of fists, and things that were coming stopped coming, or came from the sides, so she turned, and turned, and kept turning, always showing them her back.

“Got it,” Callum said.

A manhole cover. Ordinary, iron, ribbed, the kind you would walk over in any street in any town without seeing it. He got his fingers into the lifting slot and heaved and it came up an inch and fell back.

“Help me.”

“If I get off, it stops firing.”

He heaved again. It came up three inches. He got his knee under it. It came up enough. There was a ladder below, iron rungs set into concrete, going down into a dark that smelled of rust and still air.

“Go,” Ines said.

He went. She heard his boots on the rungs. She fired twice more, three times, and then she let go of the button, and the bump went dark, and the silence was enormous.

There were four of them hanging in the air at the edge of what the green had reached. Still. Their eyes were on her. All those facets.

She got off the trike and walked, not ran, to the hole, because running was what they seemed to answer. She got her feet on the ladder. She was waist deep when the nearest one jerked forward, ten metres, and stopped. She was shoulder deep when it jerked again. She reached up and got her fingers round the rim of the cover and pulled, and it was heavier than anything she had ever lifted, and it came over and fell into place above her with a sound like a cell door.

Dark. Absolute.

She climbed down by feel. Forty-one rungs. At the bottom her boots met concrete and Callum’s hand found her arm and held it hard enough to bruise.

They sat with their backs against the wall. Neither of them spoke for a long time.

“Petra,” Callum said eventually.

Ines said nothing.

Above them, something settled on the cover, and did not leave.
